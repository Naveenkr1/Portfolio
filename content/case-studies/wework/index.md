<h2>My Process</h2><p><span style="font-weight: 500;">I followed a three-stage process, with each stage feeding the next.</span></p><p>1. Research: heuristic analysis, device metrics, user journey analysis</p><p>2. Define: competitor analysis, consumer analysis, user personas, workflow analysis, a new workflow</p><p>3. Design: user interface design and prototypes<br><br></p>

<h2 id="research" data-toc-name="Research">Heuristic analysis</h2><p>I started by reviewing the live site with heatmaps and funnel behavior, looking at two numbers across the key screens: how often people clicked, and how often they moved on to the next step. The pattern was uneven. Some views had a healthy next-step rate (up to 94.16%) while their click rate was under 1% (as low as 0.79% on one view and 1.93% on another). Elsewhere, next-step and click rates both sat around 14.81%. People were moving through pages without engaging with the content that should have been persuading them.</p>

<h2>Device metrics</h2><p>The split by device was lopsided, and it told me where to focus.</p>


<div class="grid grid-1">

<p><span style="font-size: 16px;"><i>Nearly all visitors were on mobile, and that is where most of them left. The redesign therefore had to be mobile-first, with desktop treated as an equally polished second experience rather than the default.<br></i><br></span></p>

<h2>User journey analysis</h2><p>I mapped the landing page and the item page, marking what worked, what needed improvement and what failed. The main findings were:</p><p>• Users were asked to log in before seeing more details, benefits or reviews of the space.</p><p>• The mobile viewport was cluttered.</p><p>• The chatbot icon was unclear in purpose.</p><p>• The term “Bundles” was not understood.</p><p>• The team needed a deeper understanding of demand and motivation around co-working, not just the mechanics of booking.</p><p><br></p>

<h2>Competitor and consumer analysis</h2><p>I compared WeWork with three alternatives (MyHQ, Workflo and Regus), then looked at who actually buys On-Demand.</p>


<div class="grid grid-1">

![Image](/uploads/case-studies/wework/img-1792413342908-318.webp)

</div>

<p><span style="font-size: 16px;">Feature coverage showed the gap clearly. WeWork offered on-demand spaces and built-in checkout, but competitors were ahead on filtering and sorting, personalised tags and clean listings pages.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 500;"><span style="font-weight: 700;">Key takeaways:</span></span></p><p>1. The overall flow to purchase should be shortened and the persuasion increased.</p><p>2. MyHQ filters quickly within the listing page, which reduces time to purchase.</p><p>3. Workflo uses clean, minimal design with location tags and vibrant colors to speak to young professionals.</p><p>4. Regus has a clean listings page with filters and sorting, though checkout is not its priority.</p><p><br></p>

<h2>Consumer analysis</h2><p>Looking at who actually buys On-Demand:</p><p>• The top three client industries form a healthy mix of business and creative groups.</p><p>• Management is the top job profile, with individual contributors a close second, followed by CEOs and founders.</p><p>• Most On-Demand clients have or work for an incorporated business, which signals real commitment rather than casual browsing.</p><p>This told me the audience is professional and purposeful, and wants speed and confidence more than inspiration.</p>

<h2>Workflow analysis</h2><p>I mapped the existing booking workflow for each persona and found where it broke:</p><p>• “How it works” takes the user outside the journey to a separate FAQ page.</p><p>• The “Call us” call to action also pulls the user out of the flow.</p><p>• Once users leave, nothing encourages them to return or to book another space.</p>

<h2>New workflow</h2><p>I redesigned the flow to keep users inside a single, shorter path from discovery to confirmation. Information that used to live on external pages moved into the journey, filtering moved into the listing, and a clear next action was added after booking.</p>

<h2 id="design" data-toc-name="Design">Mobile</h2><p>Because 92% of traffic was on mobile, I designed that experience first.<br><h3></h3></p><ul data-lfile-container="bf41c83f-5063-47a6-ab1c-1e8f92e25efc" data-pm-slice="3 1 []"><li><strong>Browse available spaces</strong> with real photography, ratings, location, hours and a prominent “Book Day Pass” action, plus status tags such as “Quiet space available”.</li><li><strong>Bundles made understandable</strong>, with a clear price and saving shown beside the option, for example a 5-day pass or a 10-day pass, so the term explains itself.</li><li><strong>Time-slot booking</strong> for cabins and conference rooms, with seat counts and per-slot pricing visible at a glance.</li><li><strong>A clear confirmation</strong> (“You’re all set!”) with the order number and the day passes shown straight away, followed by prompts to share the pass and notify teammates.</li><li><strong>KYC as an unlock</strong>, framed as a way to get discounts and bonuses, so identity verification reads as a benefit and not a roadblock.</li></ul>

</div>

<h2>Desktop</h2><p>On desktop, I extended the same system to a larger canvas:</p><p>• A landing page that explains WeWork On-Demand, its day passes and its conference rooms, with a promotional banner for day pass bundles.</p><p>• A listings page with a richer grid of spaces.</p><p>• A detail page with a photo gallery, amenities, a map, a date picker and bundle pricing in one place.</p><p>• A confirmation page with day passes, a teammate-notification panel and help and support.</p>

<h2 id="outcome" data-toc-name="Outcome">Outcome</h2><p>The redesign delivered a mobile-first booking experience and a matching desktop experience, built on a shorter, self-contained workflow. It addresses every issue found in research: it removes the forced login before value is shown, declutters the mobile view, explains bundles clearly, and keeps users in the journey instead of sending them to external pages.</p>
